Getting around San Francisco without a car starts with a simple decision: match the transport to the part of the trip. Use the city network for local journeys, check regional rail for the airport, and leave room to explore a neighborhood on foot. San Francisco Travel's [transportation overview](https://www.sftravel.com/info/transportation-basics-how-to-get-around-san-francisco) describes the main options, including Muni, BART, walking, and ride services.

This guide explains how those choices fit together. It is a planning resource based on official information, not an account of a personally tested journey. For places to build the trip around, start with the [San Francisco destination guides](/destinations/san-francisco/).

## Start with the right system: Muni or BART?

**Muni is the city network to investigate for local sightseeing.** SFMTA's [visitor guide](https://www.sfmta.com/visitors) covers buses, Muni Metro, historic streetcars, and cable cars. A journey across town may involve more than one of these, so search for the complete trip rather than choosing a vehicle because it looks familiar.

**BART is a separate regional rail system.** Its [SFO airport guide](https://www.bart.gov/guide/airport/sfo) explains the connection between the airport and downtown San Francisco. Treat that airport journey and the final connection to the hotel as two planning questions. A train station can be convenient without being next to the front door.

Before leaving, save the accommodation's exact address, the destination address, and an alternative way to complete the journey. Check the operator's planner for the day of travel. Avoid copying a route number from an old itinerary and assuming it still operates unchanged.

## Plan the SFO arrival before the flight

BART's airport information places its SFO station in the International Terminal and describes a free AirTrain connection from the terminals. Follow the airport signage and check the operator's current directions rather than relying on a remembered terminal shortcut. The same guide links to the trip planner for departure and arrival times.

Choose the arrival option around luggage, arrival time, and the onward connection. Compare the whole airport-to-hotel journey, including transfers and walking, rather than only the rail segment. Leave some flexibility if a delayed flight could move the trip outside the service window.

BART currently describes contactless bank-card and supported mobile-wallet payment at its gates, as well as Clipper options. It instructs riders to use the same payment method when entering and leaving. Read the [current airport payment instructions](https://www.bart.gov/guide/airport/sfo) for the group before departure, especially when traveling with children or sharing a bank account. Do not assume one phone can pay for everyone in the same way.

## Choose a payment method, then compare passes

Payment advice is worth checking close to departure. SFMTA's [MuniMobile update](https://www.sfmta.com/getting-around/muni/fares/munimobile) says the app stopped selling single-ride tickets on September 1, 2026. The page describes remaining products and a gradual transition away from the standalone app. An older guide telling visitors to buy every fare in MuniMobile can therefore lead to confusion.

Check the current agency instructions for the actual fare being purchased. A payment method, a stored balance, and an unlimited-ride pass are different things; choosing one does not establish that every journey is covered. This guide deliberately avoids listing fare amounts that could go stale before the trip.

SFMTA's [Visitor Passport information](https://www.sfmta.com/fares/7-day-visitor-passport) describes one-, three-, and seven-day products covering Muni, including cable cars. It also states that the passports do not cover BART or airport transportation to and from SFO. Compare the pass with the rides actually planned, keeping the airport budget separate. Check current validity, purchase options, and prices before paying.

## Give cable cars their own place in the day

Cable cars deserve a considered slot rather than becoming the default answer to every journey. SFMTA's [cable car guide](https://www.sfmta.com/getting-around/muni/cable-cars) identifies three lines: California, Powell-Hyde, and Powell-Mason. Choose a line that fits the day's plans, then look up its boarding arrangements and payment rules.

Leave breathing room around the ride, particularly if the next activity has a timed entry. Have a different transport option available rather than making the rest of the day depend on boarding a particular car immediately. That is itinerary planning advice, not a prediction of a specific queue length.

Historic streetcars and cable cars are not interchangeable labels. The [city visitor guide](https://www.sfmta.com/visitors) lists them separately alongside buses and Metro. Check which service the route planner actually recommends before heading to a stop.

## Build walkable sections, with accessibility checked separately

A map is more useful when the day has a clear shape. San Francisco Travel describes a compact city with multiple transport options in its [getting-around guide](https://www.sftravel.com/info/transportation-basics-how-to-get-around-san-francisco). Use that as a starting point, then choose a small group of stops instead of repeatedly crossing the city for individual attractions.

Pair a neighborhood walk with a nearby meal, and keep a longer journey for the next part of the day. Check the walking route's gradient and crossings, not just its distance. A short line on a map does not establish that a route suits every traveler, stroller, or mobility aid.

SFMTA explicitly states that [cable cars lack accessible boarding](https://www.sfmta.com/getting-around/muni/cable-cars). Its [Accessibility for All resources](https://www.sfmta.com/accessibility-all) point to accessible transit information and Metro elevator status. Check the specific stops and current conditions before committing to a route; a general statement about a network is not a guarantee about an individual journey.

## Put the transport plan around the itinerary

Use the existing [48-hour San Francisco itinerary](/48-hours-in-san-francisco-the-perfect-quick-city-guide/) to choose the sights, then add transport around those choices. This article is the companion for the practical questions: how to reach the first stop, which tickets to investigate, and how to get back without depending on a car.

For example, plan one sightseeing block, identify its arrival and departure points, and check the connections before adding another block elsewhere. Keep any timed booking visible when comparing options. If the day is already crowded, remove a stop rather than treating every transfer as effortless.

Leave room for a taxi or another suitable ride when it makes the plan more manageable. Traveling without a rental car does not mean every leg must use the same form of transport. It means choosing each leg deliberately, with the official operator information close at hand.

## Before leaving the hotel

- Check the day's service information and the return journey, not just the outward route.
- Confirm that the selected fare or pass covers the service being boarded.
- Save important addresses and keep a way to pay if the phone is unavailable.
- Recheck accessibility needs and any relevant elevator status.
- Allow extra flexibility for luggage, transfers, and timed reservations.

The goal is a trip that feels comfortable to follow. Choose a few worthwhile stops, understand the transport between them, and check the current details before setting out.
